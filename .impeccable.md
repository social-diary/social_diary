## Design Context

### Users
World of Warcraft Classic players who meet people in the open world and want to remember them (PRD §4):
- **World wanderers** level alts, do quests and rares, and keep asking "who was that again?"
- **Social regulars** group with the same people for months and want reunion moments and a shared history.
- **World PvP players** run into the same enemies again and want to remember gankers, rivals and good fights.

They use the addon *inside the game*, usually in short glances between play, over a live and busy game world. The jobs:
- Look someone up fast: search, sort, filter the People list.
- Read their story: where and when you met, what you did together, your note.
- See where you crossed paths: the map.
- Add or edit an entry in seconds.

The journal is read far more often than it is edited.

### Brand Personality
**Warm, personal, timeworn.** A cherished, well-handled keepsake: friendly about the people in it, and visibly aged by use. It is a diary, not a database:
- Facts are written as prose in the diary's voice ("You first met Koko in Mulgore on 6 October…").
- Dates are relative ("today", "yesterday").
- Empty states explain what will appear and how ("Group up, trade or whisper with them, and it will be written down here").
- The tone is quiet: it never shouts during play.

### Aesthetic Direction
**A whole open book, with no window frame.**
- **Cover:** a dark leather cover with tooled gold lines, brass corner guards, and a real binding:
  - a spine with hinge grooves, which curves inward at the top and bulges outward at the bottom;
  - pages that dip into the gutter;
  - a red and gold headband;
  - binding stitches.
- **Pages:** a page stack at the outer edges, flat parchment with faint, uneven grain, and a red ribbon bookmark.
- **Lighting and wear:** everything is lit from the top left, like the game's own art. Wear and grime are slight and deliberately uneven.

**WoW identity comes from the game's own material:**
- **Fonts:** Morpheus (in capitals only) for the person's name. Friz Quadrata for everything else; small headings use Friz capitals.
- **Colour:** ink colours on parchment. Item-quality colours are re-inked so they stay readable on the light page.
- **Art:** class, race and gossip icons from the client. Index tabs sticking out of the book's edge.
- **Rubrics:** the timeline's day headings are rubricated: red capitals after a paraph (a small lozenge, lapis and red by turns) and a red rule fading out. Lapis is for ornament only, never for text that means something: blue on the page also reads Alliance.

**Built only from what the client can draw:**
- Textures, colour gradients, masks and font strings. No Blizzard frame templates (taint risk).
- Every art file is a list of candidates with a flat-colour fallback (`UI/Style.lua`, `/sd art`).

**Anti-references:**
- The alpha UI: a see-through dialog background, rows of big red buttons that all look equally important, and every line in the same small text.
- Morpheus lowercase, which turns to glyphs at small sizes.
- Inventory icons used as interface icons: they carry a dark square.
- Generic dark "gamer" panels with glowing accents.

**Theme:** light. Dark ink on warm parchment inside a dark leather cover. Pure white and GameFont gold belong only on dark surfaces.

### Good practice: the map sheet
The map view (`UI/Map/HeatSheet.lua` on `UI/Paper/Sheet.lua`, as of 8 Oct 2026) is the example to follow for new surfaces. It is a folded sheet of paper that has been carried in the book, lying across both pages and turned a degree or two. It shows the principles below working together:
- **A physical object, explained.** It has folds, creases and grime where hands opened it, and a shadow on the book. Its index tab opens and closes it, and so does a click on the book beside it. No window chrome, no close button.
- **Everything on it obeys the paper.** The map, heat, marks, border and text are all placed through the sheet's toolkit (`K.Place`, `K.Bend`, `K.Split`, `K.Light`). So whatever is printed on the paper folds, leans and takes the light with it. Nothing is drawn on top of the sheet as if it were a screen.
- **One light, physically derived.** Facets are lit by how they slope towards the top-left light. The shadow is cast from the paper's height: higher means further out and paler. The light isn't painted on. The model lives in `UI/Paper/Light.lua` (`ns.UI.Light`: the fall from a height, how a shadow shows on paper or on dark leather, the sheen across flat things), so everything that lies beside the sheet obeys the same numbers (the dungeon desk's heights: `UI/Dungeon/Heights.lua`).
- **Uneven, but stable.** Each fold misses differently, and the grime varies from piece to piece. All of it is seeded per person, so the same sheet looks the same every time it opens.
- **Restrained ink.** The border is ink at 65% (`FRAME`), so the paper shows through brown. The heat is ink bleeding into the paper. Only the last-seen X is in rubric red, so it is the one mark that stands out. A key under the map explains the marks, and other maps are text links.
- **Tunable in the open.** Every visual decision is a named, commented table at the top of its file: `FALL`, `CREASE`, `GRUNGE`, `LIFT`, `SHADOWS` in `UI/Paper/Sheet.lua`, and `PLATE`, `FRAME`, `HEAT`, `KEY` in `UI/Map/HeatSheet.lua`. Tuning means changing a number, not code.
- **Robust and optional.** It checks what the client can do, with a fallback for each:
  - Bending uses `SetVertexOffset`. Without it, the facets are still lit and the pieces are sealed against seams.
  - Rotation needs text that can turn. Without it, the sheet lies straight.
  - Text is measured at its full length, so it never gets cut short.
  - Players can turn the whole effect off with **Flat paper only**: plain paper, no folds, bends, shading, creases or grime.
- **Proven in the client.** It was built step by step against in-game screenshots, and the mock tests cover the logic: the sheet bends, the facets are lit unevenly, the shadow follows the paper, and the flat setting strips it all.

New loose sheets (the note sheet, the link sheet) are built on the same `ns.UI.LooseSheet`. They pass their own `crease` and, if they hold a text box, `straight`.

### Accessibility
PRD NFR-7, plus what the in-game tests taught us:
- **Readable type:** 13 pt body text, nothing below 11 pt, and strong ink-on-paper contrast. Readability wins over texture every time.
- **Tags:** tag colours are always paired with their text, and the presets stay distinguishable for colourblind players.
- **Scaling:** the journal window should be scalable (still to do).
- **Restrained:** the window is static, with no motion or glow to compete with gameplay.

### Design Principles
1. **Readability first, then craft.** Flat parchment beat every paper texture. Any flair (grain, grime, shading) stays below the level where it costs contrast. When in doubt, test it in the game.
2. **A book, not a window.** Every surface should be explainable as part of a physical book: cover, binding, page, ribbon, index tab, ink. Choices are text links or tabs, not buttons. Edit is a link, not an icon.
3. **Tell the story; don't list fields.** Write prose summaries, group the timeline by day, and nest a session's details under it. Let headings carry repeated information, such as "NOT MET YET", instead of repeating it on every row.
4. **Physically consistent and uneven.** One light source (top left), real perspective (the spine curves in at the top and bulges at the bottom), and wear that varies from place to place. Uniform effects read as fake.
5. **Native and robust.** Use only the game's fonts and art. Resolve art through candidate lists with fallbacks, and verify in the client with screenshots. The mock smoke test covers construction and logic only, never looks.
